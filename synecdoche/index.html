Section 1

Executive summary

The world: Dystopias don't arrive overnight. They're built one profitable decision at a time. It's the beginning of the age of the algorithm: data centres have made clean water a precious, protected resource, and corporations fight proxy wars over what's left of the rest. Europe still looks much as it does today, but algorithmic pricing has sent living costs soaring, and automation has left too few jobs to go around. At the centre of one Northern European city sits Synecdoche and the shadowy organisation behind it.

Like most people now, you're a gig worker. You've just been hired as a moderator at Synecdoche, the company that polices the city's social feeds. Its machines sort the feed, but they still can't tell a joke from a threat, or a harmless meme from a coded dog whistle, so a human makes the final call, and takes the blame when it's wrong. The game is named after the company, and after the word: a synecdoche is a part that stands for the whole. Every post you judge is one opinion, standing in for a whole person. You play one day at a time, for 30 days Provisional: a morning at the moderation desk, then an afternoon out in the city. Each morning you review a queue of posts one at a time and make a single, final call on each: Approve, Remove or Escalate. The rules change as the days go on.

What you allow through or remove shapes the city you explore, changing how its people behave and what they like. Accounts can post more than once, and some are part of the story: looking into them leads you to people and places in the city. The afternoon is free roam: you ride a bike around a compact district of a Northern European city and choose what to do. You deliver food for extra money and see what your decisions did to the city.

Then, on Day 7 Provisional, someone else gets into your queue. A hacker group reaches you through the moderation screen itself. Their contact opens up the afternoon missions and, later, hands you a botnet of your own: a network of fake social-media accounts that post at scale to sway what the city thinks. Until they reach you, the afternoon is just delivery work for extra money. From then on you're walking a line: the company pays your wages and watches your numbers, while the hackers offer the truth and ask for something in return.

One more post. One more delivery. One more clue.

The "I caused that" moment Provisional
The player sees a moderation decision become a lived consequence, either out in the city or in the next day's feed.
Pitch shorthand
Papers, Please for social-media moderation. This is only a comparison for pitching, not a blueprint.
Pressure Approved
Time and money, and both come from the company. As the days go on, the company adds time pressure to the shift and mistakes cost more. Meanwhile, algorithmically set rent and living costs keep rising. Pay comes from moderation accuracy and deliveries. In the late game, most of the money comes from the botnet.
Losing Provisional
Mistakes cost you. Repeated wrong calls bring fines out of your pay, and too many get you fired by Synecdoche, which ends the run. Money works the same way: you can keep playing in debt for a while, but three days in a row in debt costs you your home and ends the run. Falling behind also forces harder trade-offs, such as another delivery shift instead of a hacker mission. These rules are preliminary, and the numbers may change.
Winning Approved
Reach a scripted ending. The first release runs 30 days Provisional, and the story ends on the last one. There is more than one ending, and which one you reach depends on your choices. Provisional The main axis is whether you back the company or the hackers.
Section 2

Four pillars, four mini-games Approved

The pillars are decision tests: any feature that doesn't deepen one of them, or connect two of them, stays out. The four mini-games are what the player actually plays.

P1

Moderation & Judgement

Read a post, weigh policy and context, and make one final call.

Passes if decisions are hard because of context, not because there are lots of buttons.

P2

Investigation & Context

Spend limited access to see who is behind a post and who they're connected to.

Passes if investigation feeds a lead without handing over the answer.

P3

Urban Work & Exploration

Explore a compact city that earns you extra money and hides the leads.

Passes if the district is fun to cross and every afternoon system reuses it.

P4

Influence & Manipulation

Use fake accounts to push what the city believes, and fight rival botnets for control of it.

Passes if the player can see their influence out in the world, and feels what it costs them at the desk.

Section 3 · Player-facing actions and loop

Game Mechanics

A day in the life

Every in-game day is a self-contained loop that then repeats: a morning moderation shift, an afternoon in the city, and a short evening summary. It follows the same daily rhythm as Stardew Valley, where each day is one complete cycle and the next one builds on it.

  1. Morning · 4–6 minModeration shiftWork through the queue, investigate when something's off
  2. Afternoon · 6–8 minThe cityDeliver for extra money, follow the hackers' leads or run your botnet
  3. Evening · ~1 minSummaryPay, rent, what changed because of you
  4. Next dayHarderNew rules, new tools, costlier rent

The moderation desk Approved

  • One post on screen at a time. Arrow keys or a controller move between actions and Space/Enter confirms. The mouse also works.
  • Three final actions: Approve, Remove, Escalate. Escalate opens six reasons: Threat/Violence, Self-Harm/Crisis, Harassment/Abuse, Fraud/Scam, Privacy/Sensitive Info and Misinformation/Public Harm. Where it's enabled, you can also escalate a post as Needs Review when no reason fits. It still counts as Escalate, not a fourth action.
  • Decisions are final. The post leaves the queue and there's no going back to change it.
  • The rules are always to hand. A policy guide sits beside the queue and explains each action and escalation reason in plain words. It updates as the company's rules change, and the supervisor flags each change at the start of the day.
  • Immediate, legible scoring. In the current prototype a correct call scores +5 and a correct escalation with the right reason +6. The wrong reason or the wrong action scores −4, missing a serious escalation −5, and escalating something that didn't need it −1.
  • The shift talks back. The Queue Assistant (an authored in-game helper character, not an AI) and your supervisor interrupt with authored dialogue, and later, so do the hackers. Input locks until they finish.
  • The clock is the company's. From Day 7 the whole shift is on a timer, not each post. Run out of time and you simply get paid less: posts left in the queue go unpaid, and nothing else happens to them. Later shifts get tighter, and a wrong call costs more than it did in week one.
  • Shifts grow from 20 posts on Day 1 to 28 by Day 13.

Escalate → Misinformation / Public Harm. The author's intent stays unclear. Investigation gives the player evidence, not a verdict.

  • Basic Investigation shows the profile and recent public activity, never future posts. It is logged for the current shift only.
  • Deep Investigation Approved gives 3 free per shift; each extra one counts as a data-protection breach and is fined by the company. You can spend one on an account that turns out not to matter. Going deep reveals the account's fictional IP address and the other accounts linked to it. This is where the screen breaks into the hacker layer. A shared IP is a lead, not proof: some links are innocent, like a shared flat or a café.
  • Any account can be investigated, and most reveal little. Important threads get soft signposting rather than quest markers.

The hackers reach in Approved

First contact happens on the moderation queue, through the dialogue screen, the same overlay the Queue Assistant uses. It arrives in the hacker's terminal style instead of the company's. Moderation locks while they talk and resumes when they're done.

Their contact is what leads to the afternoon missions. The missions are the story's beats. Each one is a hand-written chapter that moves the plot forward, shows you more of Synecdoche and the shadowy organisation behind it, and steers you towards one of the endings. Following leads is a hacker activity: without them, you just deliver for extra money. Later, the hackers also hand you a botnet. From there the player has to walk the line between hacker and corporate. The company controls your pay, your time and your job. The hackers control what you get to find out, and their missions fight back against the rise of Synecdoche and the shadowy organisation behind it. They make first contact on Day 7. Provisional

  • Approved There is one major hacker group in the story. Provisional It makes first contact on Day 7.
  • Open What the hackers ask for at the desk, and what they give back.
  • Approved The line is invisible. The game tracks where you stand with each side, but never shows you a meter. Open What leaning too far either way costs.

The botnet: a gift from the hackers Approved

One network of bots, used in two active modes that share the same roster, plus a Mode C that is deferred for now. You run it from the PC in your flat. It is the late game's main source of money and influence. The botnet creates fake social-media users and posts at scale to sway what the city's inhabitants think. It can also be turned against the existing infrastructure of the world. Provisional

  • How it pays Provisional Clients pay you to push their products, trends and causes on the turf you control. The more influence you hold, the more they pay.
  • Why it matters at the desk. Your own bots' posts turn up in your moderation queue. Letting them through counts as a wrong call at work, but your network gains influence. Remove them and you keep your job clean but weaken your network.
  • Where the money goes. Botnet money pays the hackers for bigger missions, which uncover more of how Synecdoche and the shadowy organisation behind it really work.

Mode A · Management

Approved

Recruit bots and organise them into botnets that gain influence over the topics and areas of cyberspace you want to occupy: your turf. It's a business-management mini-game (reference: Yakuza: Like a Dragon), similar in spirit but not a copy.

  • Each piece of turf has three metrics, and each needs to reach a minimum threshold before the turf pays. Pushing past the thresholds pays more.
  • Your bots raise the metrics. Each bot is stronger in some metrics than others, and some bots suit certain kinds of turf better.
  • The payout is money and influence, and the results show up in the game world.
Reach how many people see it Credibility whether it looks like real people Buzz how much it gets talked about

Working names The metrics are working ideas and may change.

Mode B · Botnet Wars

Approved

Your botnets go out and fight rival botnets, run by rival hacking groups or by the authorities, for a bigger share of the turf, and winning makes your network more influential. It's a squad-deployment auto-battler (reference: MGSV's Combat Deployment): pick a squad from the bots you've recruited and send it out. The hands-off battle is drawn as an exaggerated physical fight.

Win More share of the turf, more influence and more for Mode A to manage.

Lose Mode A takes the hit: you lose bots, lose influence and earn less money. Meanwhile the authorities and rival hacking groups consolidate their power and influence.

Mode C · Takedown

Deferred for now

A defensive battle against the authorities (reference: Yakuza's shareholder meetings). It's the weakest of the three modes, so it's parked for now, and the moderation queue covers its job (see below).

Not in the first release. Kept on record in case it comes back later.

  • Your day job can give you away Approved direction The authorities find your botnet through your moderation work. A wrong call means breaking the company's rules. Repeated wrong calls bring fines, and enough of them draw the authorities' attention to your botnet. Once they're on to it, they damage it directly. This is how the authorities threaten you while Mode C is deferred. Open The exact thresholds, and whether it's in the first release.
  • Your botnet changes the city Provisional When your bots push a topic hard enough, the world starts to reflect it. More posts about it arrive in your own moderation queue, posters for it go up on the streets, people in the city start talking about it, and related products appear in the shops.

Afternoon: the bike & the district Approved

The afternoon is free roam. Nothing forces a route or a task: you can ride anywhere in the district, take deliveries, follow a hacker lead, run the botnet or just explore.

A delivery works like this: accept an order, ride to the restaurant, collect it, pick a route, deliver, and get paid and rated. Then the next tempting offer arrives. The fun comes from knowing routes and shortcuts, unreliable restaurants, stacking orders and the platform's algorithmic nudges. Failing costs you tips, a streak or a complaint, never a Game Over. Delivery is always available; it's how you make extra money before, and alongside, the hackers.

Consequences show up in the district itself: the view from the window, news, graffiti, adverts, NPC dialogue and the next day's queue.

Money & tools Approved

Rent, food, transport and utilities keep rising, and algorithms set the prices. It is the game's commentary on the capitalism the company stands for: the same system that grades your work also prices your life. Spare money has two uses, and they pull in opposite directions:

  • Tools for the day job that expose, highlight or organise information; they never make the call for you. Open The first release picks one to three, from candidates such as Signal Lens, Dog-Whistle Glossary, Domain Watch and Public Profile Peek.

At the end of every shift, your wages raise the same question: keep the company happy, or bankroll the people trying to bring it down.

  • Paying for missions Approved The hackers may be idealists, but their operations don't run on goodwill. You pay for the resources they need, and in return you unlock bigger, more daring afternoon missions against Synecdoche and the shadowy organisation behind it. Each one reveals a little more about how the world really works.

Every scripted mission uses the same seven beats Approved

  1. TriggerA hacker request, often built on something you found at the desk
  2. BriefA clear goal and a reason to care
  3. PrepareChoose tools, information, route and risk
  4. PlayA short deterministic activity in the same district
  5. OutcomeSuccess, partial or failure, with a readable cause
  6. ConsequenceMoney, evidence, influence, NPCs or the world
  7. ReturnBack into the daily loop with no dead end
Section 4 · Design choice

Look & feel: one company, two interfaces Approved

The company's own screens use a calm corporate style, and anything the hackers touch switches to a raw terminal style. The change of style tells the player whose system they are in. The tone is grounded satire: every post is written or approved by a human, so the queue pokes fun at real online life while taking real harm seriously.

Section 5 · What each agent does, seen through play

AI Architecture

No AI runs while you play. Every agent works during development, and the player meets what it made. Each card says when the agent runs during development, then what the player sees in the game because of it.

Rules & QA Agent

In development · runs when the designer approves a decision, and on every change

In the game: Rules that behave the same everywhere (a correct escalation scores +6 on the desk and shows +6 in the evening summary) and a shift that never breaks.

Desk & Day Engineer

In development · runs when a desk, investigation or day-cycle rule is approved

In the game: The one-post desk, "Investigate account" revealing @NauseaNotes_17's earlier posts, the shift clock, and the evening summary with wages, rent and the debt warning.

City & Mission Engineer

In development · runs when a part of the district or a mission is approved

In the game: The bike, the shortcuts, the restaurants and delivery doors, and the hacker missions that play out in the same streets.

Botnet Engineer

In development · runs when botnet rules are approved

In the game: Turf cards with Reach, Credibility and Buzz metrics, the squad screen, and Botnet Wars results.

Consequence Engineer

In development · runs when a desk, mission or botnet outcome is defined

In the game: The "I caused that" moment: the posters outside change, the window shows a calm, rioting or burning city, and tomorrow's queue brings more of what you let through.

Content & Art Agent

In development · runs when a content or visual brief is approved

In the game: New posts and recurring accounts in the queue, plus the corporate and hacker screens and the city's sprites, all reviewed by the designer.

Section 6

Scope: a compact first release Approved

The goal is a complete, shippable game in seven weeks, six of them still to go. The four systems are not equally deep; moderation carries the most weight.

Where the build is today
An early work-in-progress Unity prototype, not yet at vertical-slice stage. It is the morning desk only. The loop runs: one post at a time, Approve/Remove/Escalate, keyboard controls, scoring, feedback, dialogue interruptions and a shift summary. It has 213 approved posts and one Day 8 investigation encounter, which runs through a developer test setup. Deep Investigation is partly built. The linked-accounts view is a placeholder screen.
Not built yet
The afternoon city, bike and deliveries, money and rent, tools, missions, hacker contact beyond one interruption, consequences, endings, and a save state that has been verified. Most of the remaining six weeks goes on turning that prototype into the first complete loop.

In the first release

  • The full moderation desk, using the approved post library
  • Posts from one social platform, text only
  • Account investigation, including linked accounts through Deep Investigation
  • The botnet: Management and Botnet Wars
  • One compact district, with bike deliveries
  • 3–5 scripted missions Provisional
  • A beginning, an escalation and multiple endings
  • Save/load, settings, feedback and a stable build

Not yet Deferred

  • Stealth/infiltration, and other mini-games such as phishing
  • More districts and a 60–80-day campaign
  • Multiple social platforms, or image-post moderation
  • Endless moderation mode

Ship rule: no new major systems after the first playable end-to-end build. If a week slips, cut in this order: linked accounts shrink to one authored lead → missions drop from 5 to 3 → tools drop from 3 to 1 → Botnet Wars (Management stays). Never cut: the desk, one hacker mission, one visible consequence.

> how Synecdoche gets built

AI agents work only during development. They produce code, candidate content, documentation and test reports. Nothing reaches the player until the designer has reviewed it, and the shipped game makes no AI calls.

AI development pipeline

Content is the one pipeline that already runs end to end. Posts are generated on a local model at £0 cost, and none enter the game without review. It already runs overnight on the development PC, producing up to 40 candidate posts a night for review the next morning, so growing the library for a 30-day campaign costs review time, not writing time:

generation brief ─▶ local model (LM Studio) ─▶ candidate JSON
      ─▶ schema + policy validators ─▶ HUMAN REVIEW (Streamlit dashboard)
      ─▶ build_content_library_v4.ps1 ─▶ approved library (213 posts)
      ─▶ Unity StreamingAssets/ModerationContent/approved_posts.json
no auto-approve · no auto-merge · no auto-publish · story accounts never generated as filler

Art pipeline

  • Existing assets first. The city's tilemap and its NPCs come from pixel-art packs the project already owns: a tileset with road pieces for the district, and 19 NPC characters, most with full four-direction walk cycles
  • New art is supervised. Anything missing is made with PixelLab, an AI pixel-art tool, then finished by hand in Aseprite so it matches the style

Agents hand off to each other through a shared Markdown Agent Handoff folder in the Obsidian vault, and the designer routes all work. Claude Code and Codex run the code and documentation agents; a local model runs bulk content.

Coordination: agents don't talk to each other directly, and there is no MCP bus. They share state through a Markdown Agent Handoff folder in the Obsidian vault (CURRENT_STATE, HANDOFF_LOG, OPEN_QUESTIONS). The designer routes all work and approves every creative decision. Tools today: Claude Code and Codex for code and documentation, local models for bulk content.

Prompt constraints (every agent, every run)

  • Load only the agent brief, the relevant GDD section and directly linked notes, never the whole vault
  • Label every claim as Approved, Provisional, Open or Deferred, and never promote one yourself
  • Add nothing that isn't in the task brief. Scope questions go to OPEN_QUESTIONS
  • Prefer deterministic, authored data over anything generated at runtime
  • Stop after the requested validation. List every file changed and why
  • Only call something "working" if it was tested in Unity Play Mode

Engine integration

  • Unity 2022.3.33f1, with scenes MainMenu and Question. Content loads as JSON from StreamingAssets, and Day 0 falls back to Resources/posts.json
  • Posts and accounts are separate: postId ≠ accountId. Story accounts use stable IDs such as ACCOUNT-STORY-NAUSEA-001, and filler accounts use ACCOUNT-FILLER-…
  • An account's visible history may only contain posts published before today. Future posts are never exposed
  • Dev overrides (start day, Day 8 fixture, tutorial reset) exist but are switched off in every commit
  • The Unity repo and the Obsidian vault are separate Git repositories and are committed separately

Biggest constraint Provisional

My biggest constraint is fixed AI capacity at £0 extra spend, because development runs on a ChatGPT Plus plan and an existing Claude subscription. Their usage caps, not money, limit how much agent work fits into a week. If the caps ever block the ship plan, the upgrade path is ChatGPT Pro (5×).

  • Every task is bounded: a token band, a stop condition, and a human who ends the run
  • Context is kept small: the agent brief plus linked notes, and the handoff files so no agent re-derives the project
  • Bulk content generation runs on local models at £0
  • Upgrade path: ChatGPT Pro (5×) only if the caps block the ship plan

API constraints

  • No API calls at runtime. The shipped game runs fully offline, and no API key ships with it
  • Development uses subscriptions, not API keys: ChatGPT Plus (Codex) and a Claude plan. There is no pay-per-token bill, so the limits are each plan's usage caps and rate limits, which reset on a schedule
  • Bulk content runs on a local model (LM Studio): no cap and no cost, but lower quality, so every post is reviewed by a human
  • When a cap is hit, the run stops, and work either waits for the reset or moves to the other tool. API prices are used only as a yardstick for estimates

Each core development task (one bounded agent run) costs approximately 21k tokens, so 108 runs over seven weeks would cost approximately $4 at Claude Haiku 4.5 pricing ($1 per million input tokens and $5 per million output, assuming 80% input; API prices are in US dollars, and any real spend would be in pounds). Allowing about 4× for agents re-reading context each turn, it's about $17. For comparison: Sonnet 5 would cost about $8–33 and Opus 5 about $21–83. Actual extra spend: £0, because the work is covered by existing subscriptions.

Against the usage caps, the same 4× applies: about 9M tokens of cap use over seven weeks, roughly 1.3M a week, shared between the two plans. The designer's own time is the other bottleneck, since every run ends with a human review or a Play Mode test. Open Hours per week, to be measured in Week 1.

Each generated moderation post uses about 1k input and 0.5k output tokens, roughly $0.0035 at Haiku pricing. It currently costs nothing because it runs locally. After each run, log the model, task, context size, turns, retries, files changed and why it stopped, then compare estimates with actuals in a weekly retrospective.
